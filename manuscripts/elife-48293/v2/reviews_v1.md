## Review text

DOI: [10.7554/eLife.48293.sa1](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.48293.sa1)

In the interests of transparency, eLife publishes the most substantive revision requests and the accompanying author responses.

Acceptance summary:

This study reports two experiments seeking to investigate the neural representations of newly instructed rules. Humans are able to receive instructions and then carry out new rules immediately. This ability to rapidly represent and carry out stimulus-response rules is a central aspect of human flexible behavior. However, little is known about how the brain represents newly instructed rules. The present fMRI study provides evidence that the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex codes newly instructed rules within the first two trials, that this coding is stable while univariate activity decreases, and that changes in VLPFC-striatal connectivity increases.

Decision letter after peer review:

Thank you for submitting your article "Initial neural representation of individual rules after first-time instruction" for consideration by eLife. Your article has been reviewed by three peer reviewers, and the evaluation has been overseen by David Badre as the Reviewing Editor and Timothy Behrens as the Senior Editor. The following individuals involved in review of your submission have agreed to reveal their identity: Apoorva Bhandari (Reviewer #1); Alexandra Woolgar (Reviewer #3).

The reviewers have discussed the reviews with one another and the Reviewing Editor has drafted this decision to help you prepare a revised submission.

The reviewers were in agreement that the topic of this set of experiments – the neural coding of newly instructed rules – is of high importance and potential impact. They also felt that the study was overall well-designed and carefully conducted. However, there were also several conclusions drawn in the paper that the reviewers felt were either poorly supported or would require additional analyses to support. We have consolidated these consensus concerns into the following list of essential revisions.

Essential revisions:

1) The most important claim of this paper is that neural representations of novel stimulus-response rules are established after instructions (or after the first implementation – see point 3 below). There is an alternative account of these findings that has not been ruled out, however. The pattern similarity effects may also reflect stimulus-specific episodic retrieval. With reference to the example trials shown in Figure 1, when subjects see the cue "water" on trial 3, they may be retrieving their episodic memory of the last time they saw the cue "water" and what they did in that context. Such a pattern of episodic retrieval would also predict a similarity structure consistent with that observed in the VLPFC in this study. Moreover, such episodic retrieval is unlikely in the control condition in Experiment 2 when the response is cued on every trial, thus predicting the absence of such a similarity structure. There is a deflationary version of this alternative that is problematic for the main claims here. Specifically, we know that people can retrieve items from LTM after one exposure to them. We also know that systems including VLPFC are important for this kind of retrieval. So, if rule implementation is distinct from this kind of episodic retrieval, then this would be problematic. Thus, it is important to either rule out this alternative, or discuss its implications. One way to potentially rule it out would be to show pattern similarity effects between different rules in the task that would only be predicted by the SR contingencies, but not by stimulus-based episodic retrieval.

2) Claims of the stability of the representation are overstated, unless they are supported with additional analysis. The pattern analysis approach employed in this study, though elegant, cannot support such a claim. This approach detects rule-specific, structured pattern similarity in sets of 2 trials (e.g. same-rule similarity > different-rule similarity across repetitions 1 and 2 or 3 and 4 in Experiment 1). While this provides evidence for rule-identity specific pattern coding across adjacent pairs, it does not provide evidence that the same rule identity-specific patterns are stably maintained across the first 4 (or first 8 in Experiment 2) trials. In theory, one way this issue could be addressed would be to analyze identity specific patterns in overlapping pairs of repetitions (1 and 2, 2 and 3, 3 and 4). Unfortunately, such an analysis is likely to be biased because of the way the trial sequences were constructed in order to enable analysis in each learning stage (i.e. using two, independent, 'atomic' 8-trial sequences). Therefore, without a control analysis (using realistic autocorrelated noise) or an alternative analysis approach that can provide more direct evidence, the claim of stable representations is not supported.

3) The primary claim that rule identity-specific representations are established immediately after instruction implies that something was tested about the representation of the rule once instructed but not yet implemented. This was not tested, however. Only data from implementation was analyzed. One reviewer further noted that to support the claim that the representation is established from the very first implementation then it is necessary to show that the identify-specific representations are above chance even for the 1 and 2 comparison specifically. This would establish that there is a consistency in representation between first and second implementation. This is as close as possible to establishing that it is present from the first use. The absence of an interaction with learning stage is not sufficient to establish a significant effect at all learning stages. Analysis of the instruction phase itself might also be helpful in establishing these early effects. In the absence of tests prior to implementation, then this claim should be revised.

4) The paper implies that explicitly learnt rules are contrasted with incidentally learnt ones to isolate processes associated with following explicit instructions. But, this is not what has been done. Instead, they contrast two conditions which both involve explicit instructions (previously learnt or currently given). Relatedly, since all analyses are for activity associated with rule implementation, and not learning per se, some rephrasing would help improve clarity. E.g. this was seen for implementation of previously instructed, and not currently instructed, rules?

5) The control for stimulus or response coding rather than rule coding largely comes from a lack of effect in the control condition. Beyond the potential weakness of arguing from a null, it is not clear why S-R rules would not be incidentally represented even though they are instructed on every trial in the control task. Thus, if one assumes that they are represented, then, in fact, the 'control task' results would suggest a lack of replication. Some discussion of this difference is merited.

6) An alternative explanation for the lack of effect of difficulty (ns trend towards increased rule coding under difficult conditions) is that there is an increased representation of the more difficult rules on the correct trials averaging out against poorer representation on error trials. There is precedence for this (Woolgar et al., 2016). To confirm which explanation applies, the analysis should be repeated using only correct trials. If the increase is still in the same direction or is stronger, then the 'knowing but not doing' explanation cannot hold, and the explanation should instead be an adaptive response in which more processing resources are allocated to more difficult tasks.

7) The searchlight analysis appears biased and can be dropped.

8) The pattern analysis approach used in the study is elegant, but it immediately brings to mind several concerns about the possibility of bias due to regressor correlation or temporal noise autocorrelation in fMRI. Reviewers had to carefully review the results in the referenced earlier paper (Ruge et al., 2018) in order to be satisfied that the results observed here are not due to bias. It will be very helpful to the reader if the argument about the unbiased nature of the method is recapitulated in summary in the current manuscript. This is particularly the case given the known difficulty with PFC MVPA – readers may be skeptical of the method without knowing some of the results from Ruge et al., 2018.

First, the concern over the episodic retrieval account of the findings remains unaddressed, either through an analysis or through discussion. The reviewers remain concerned that this is an important alternative; they were not persuaded by the response. After discussion, there was agreement that this alternative needs to be addressed more directly. Ideally, it could be ruled out in the data with an additional control analysis. We are pessimistic that this will be possible, particularly as it may boil down to a question of whether this representation in VLPFC is causal rapid behavior; something that can't be addressed with fMRI. However, in the absence, of a new result that argues against episodic retrieval, this point must be addressed in the Discussion, with caveats as appropriate.

Second, the reviewers agreed that the paper continues to make claims that appear stronger than their empirical support.

- There is agreement that the study does not show rule coding prior to the first implementation of trials, so language implying that this is the case should be revised.

- Changing the definition of stability has not fully addressed this concern for reviewers. Again, the language should be revised to make this clear.

- The implicit (non-instructed) versus instructed distinction remains unclear for reviewers.

- Claims that the study looks at "novel combinations of elements without reference to the elements themselves" should be revised, as reviewers saw no evidence of this.

Finally, the reviewers have requested to see the ME of instruction type in VLPFC so that a representation of a rule can be distinguished from a stimulus.

To be sure that these issues in clarity are fully conveyed, I have put the reviewers full comments in the letter below. Again, these points appear fully addressable by revision to the text, but they are essential to move forward with the paper.

Reviewer #1:

I have now evaluated the revised manuscript and the author's response to reviewers. The manuscript is significantly improved. However, I do think one significant issue has been left unaddressed – this is described in point 1 below. This is the only crucial issue as far as I'm concerned – but it is make-or-break. Additionally, I have made some suggestions for changes in language. The numbers below refer to the original comments.

1) I don't think the authors have adequately addressed the first concern that was brought up in the reviews. I'm not persuaded that the authors have clarified why the identity-specific pattern similarity effect should be interpreted as a representation of individual rules at all instead of just a reflection of a stimulus-cued retrieval of the last episode involving the stimulus from long-term memory. From reading the authors' response, they seem to be assuming that these are one and the same thing. However, if the subject was simply retrieving the episode when the same stimulus was last presented (even if, for instance in the extreme, they responded to the stimulus randomly) they could get the same pattern of results. I don't see why that should be interpreted as a rule representation. A rule representation should code a stimulus-response link, at the very minimum. There is no evidence that the specific pattern similarity reflects the coding of a stimulus-response link or that it contains any information about the response whatsoever. At the very least, they need to acknowledge this alternative account in the Discussion.

2) The authors continue to use the term 'stability of coding'. The authors now explicitly define what they mean by stability and have clearly specified the nature of their claim and that is satisfactory. However, the use of the term stability is still susceptible to misinterpretation. Therefore, I would strongly suggest that they consider the following formulation to minimize the misinterpretation of their findings by readers:

instead of "stability of rule representations" use "stable presence of rule representations"

3) The authors have carried out additional formal comparisons which show that the identity-specific coding can be measured as early as the first pair of trials. This certainly strengthens their claim. Moreover, it is clear from the author's responses that they do not wish to make any claims about rule coding prior to the first implementation of trials – so we are all on the same page. However, I do think the language used to describe this claim is still problematic and susceptible to misinterpretation. I, therefore, suggest the following three edits that would make the language more acceptable:

Abstract:

"VLPFC representations were established right after first-time instruction and remained stable across 10 early implementation trials.

Replace with:

"VLPFC representations were detectable as early as the first implementation trial and remained stable across 10 trials."

Discussion:

"The key finding from the time-resolved MVPA is that rule identity-specific representations were established immediately after the first-time instruction of completely novel S-R rules and continued to be involved throughout the first few implementation trials. "

Replace with:

"The key finding from the time-resolved MVPA is that rule identity-specific representations of completely novel S-R rules were detectable as early as the first implementation trial and continued to be involved throughout the first few trials."

General Conclusions: delete the word "instantaneously".

Reviewer #2:

I would like to thank the authors for the extensive responses to our previous concerns. While some aspects have been addressed very nicely in the revision, I believe some responses do not yet fully resolve the core issues.

1) I am still not convinced that the authors have clarified the role of episodic memory. The authors state that the experiment was not designed to test for the difference between different forms of memory, but this does not seem to be the main point of the criticism. Rather, I believe the important difference lies in whether the findings reflect an episodic memory representation that refers to past events, or a more a "task set" like representation that controls attention and behaviour. Moreover, the authors proceed with their argument based on the idea that the alternative hypothesis pertains to "retrieval of S-R links implemented at least once". My concern was about retrieval of episodic memories made during the instruction phase.

To move forward, I therefore suggest to investigate pattern similarity effects between different rules in the task that would only be predicted by the SR contingencies, but not by stimulus-based episodic retrieval, as suggested before. In addition, discussing the potential difference between an episodic memory representation and a task set representation would be useful.

2) I am also not fully happy with the reply to point 2. Changing the definition of stability does not really seem to do the issue justice. In my opinion, the data does not show that the representation of a specific rule remains the same thought the implementation phase, as the term stability clearly suggests. Rather, the representation that is established early could change over the task, either gradual, or even entirely between trials 2 and 3, and the results would look the same. So I believe the claim of stable representations is not supported.

3) Finally, I still don't see how the use of the word incidental is warranted (Q4). The authors cite evidence that the two processes in Experiment 1 and 2 are different, which I believe. Yet, incidental usually refers to memories that are acquired without intention, and I don't think this is the case in the control condition of Experiment 2. Rather, there may be no memory of the SR rules at all, or at least it is not used when the buttons are pressed in the implementation phase. I therefore would suggest to use a different term.

Reviewer #3:

I don't feel that the authors have done enough to tone down their claims in response to our concerns. In particular:

• They argue that it is clear in the title "Initial neural representation of individual rules after first-time instruction'" that the conclusions refer to subsequent implementation of instructed rules (response to substantive comment 3). To me this is not clear. The would do better, then to say "from first implementation"?

• The authors have not revised their claim that they are studying the transition from 'knowing what to do' to 'actually doing it' (Abstract), nor the claim that they contrast "instructed" to "incidental" learning (Abstract). These claims were majors concern in the first review, and have not, as far as I can see, been addressed.

• The change in wording in the Abstract from "this was confined to" to "this was found preferentially for".… "instructed stimulus learning in contrast to incidental learning", does nothing to deal with the concern raised. It still implies that they contrast explicitly learnt rules with incidentally learnt ones to isolate process associated with following explicit instructions – but this is not what they have done. Instead they contrast two conditions which both involve explicit instructions (previously learnt or currently given). The description in the Introduction is now clearer, but needs revising in the Abstract also to avoid misleading the reader. Moreover, as the authors now mention, it is quite possible that the S-R rules may not be encoded at all in Experiment 2 (they can simply press the instructed button each time, and never learn any S-R associations). The authors cannot claim to contrast instructed to incidental, when in fact they admit themselves that it's really "instructed" vs. "incidental or maybe not at all".

Regarding the response to our substantive comments in decision letter:

1) The authors argument seems to be that the rule has not been implemented on the first presentation, so an episodic memory of the implementation cannot explain pattern similarity between the first and subsequent presentations. This argument does not completely refute the concern – it could still be that the pattern associated with memory for this episode on subsequent presentations is similar to the pattern associated with actually performing the implementation on the first presentation. However, it does seem a more natural conclusion to think that the similarity would be driven mainly by the repeated implementation (which is the same each time) rather than a memory (which is different on the first trial).

2) The new analysis (subsection “MVPA (collapsed across experiments 1 and 2)”) of pattern similarity for phases 1-2 helps with the claim that rule – specific patterns are present from the first presentation and that rules continue to be coded across implementation stages. It was good to see this included.

I would, however, still take issue with the wording of the rule representations as "stable", despite the caveats in the Discussion, as the stability of the representation was not tested.

Additionally, in the Results subsection “MVPA (experiment 2)”, the ANOVA shows ME of instruction type that was modulated by region (stronger for VLPFC) – this should be followed up by showing the ME of instruction type in each region separately (crucially: it must be above chance for VLPFC, if we want to conclude about rules rather than stimuli). It would then be important to show that this is so in the early implementation stage specifically, in order for claims about rule representation (rather than stimulus) in the first implementation, to hold up.

3) I still feel they should update the wording (see above), we still have no evidence that they are established immediately after instruction, just that they are consistent between first and second use. This is important, as planning to do something and actually doing it may be quite different processes.

4) The authors do not seem to have made any revision to the text/terminology based on this concern, but neither do they refute the concern in their response.

5) I think we were asking for more discussion of this point in the manuscript, I do not think they have added any.

6) Good to see this analysis included.

7) I don't too much mind inclusion of searchlights to check for missed regions or as further description of the ROI results – and I think it's useful to include that the same result pertained to visual and sensorimotor cortex, given concerns over stimulus and response confounds. But I do feel it's a bit much to give both whole brain searchlights and then the exact same searchlights again with results restricted to within the ROIs. Presumably this was so that they can be shown at a lower threshold and there are few multiple comparisons within the smaller mask? Certainly, in any case the authors cannot claim that the "ROI-based findings were confirmed by searchlight-based MVPAs" – this does not confirm anything since the data are double dipped. The authors would also need to be much more careful about presenting this as a description of the results already known to pertain to these regions (and really, it should be given as a figure, in this case). If, for example, they used the searchlights to suggest that within a particular ROI it was a particular part of the ROI (e.g., the posterior part) that seemed to hold the most rule information, that would be interesting as it would give us more resolution on the effect. As it is, I don't think it's adding anything useful – the searchlights seem to be used mainly to imply that there's more decoding in left than right VLPFC, but this is not directly tested and anyway if that was the case the ROI ANOVA should have revealed that (effect of region).

8) Fine.

Additional comments:

Introduction: "it remains unknown whether similar regions also code novel combinations of familiar task elements (here: nouns, button presses) without reference to the representations of the individual task elements themselves." And identical text beginning "we identified" in Discussion. I don't see how the authors can claim to have looked at this at all, since their decoding is always confounded with these task elements. Yes, coding is stronger in Experiment 1 than Experiment 2, which also has those elements, but that does not mean that the code in Experiment 1 is without reference to those elements. It could simply be that the elements are coded more strongly, and in relationship to one another, in Experiment 1.

I am somewhat concerned that so many of their references seem to be erroneous. I pointed out quite a few seemingly incorrect/inappropriate references in the first submission and was surprised to see erroneous references in this revision. I think the reference we gave them at one point in the summarised review letter was wrong (we pointed them to Woolgar et al., 2016 instead of 2015), but I still feel that they ought to have read the paper and worked this out, rather than blindly citing it. It makes me worry slightly about the rest of their references.
