Thank you for choosing to send your work entitled “Quantification of gait parameters in freely walking wild type and sensory deprived Drosophila melanogaster” for consideration at eLife. Your article has been evaluated by a Senior Editor and 2 reviewers, one of whom is a member of our Board of Reviewing Editors. The following individuals responsible for the peer review of your submission want to reveal their identity: Ronald L Calabrese, Reviewing Editor.

The Reviewing Editor and the other reviewer discussed their comments before we reached this decision, and the Reviewing Editor has assembled the following comments based on the two reports.

The authors develop very sophisticated behavioral technology and software for the analysis of insect walking focusing their experimental effort on the rich genetic system Drosophila. Their automated optical method for tracking insect footfalls and analyzing the kinematic data are powerful and impressive. The data acquired is extensive and is mined for many pertinent locomotor parameters including interlimb coordination and gait. Data is presented that gait and some other characteristics, such as stance linearity and footprint alignment, are different in flies walking slower and faster speeds. There are not discrete transitions in gait, however: the typical tripod gait occurs at all speeds, but with diminishing frequency in slower flies. The normative data set is extensive and of real value for future analysis in this important genetic system. The most interesting aspect of the work involves inactivating sensory neurons of the legs; one set of experiments uses a mutation that specifically disrupts the function of chrodotonal organs, while a second set of experiments uses a combinatorial misexpression approach to inactivate the majority of sensory neurons in the leg.

These experiments give a surprising result; sensory deficient flies walked slower than wild-type flies, but maintained a typical tripod gait with normal left–right and interlimb coordination. Stance traces showed some defects, however: step length increased with a more wobbly body placement and a larger variability in footprint clustering. The surprise here comes because in the well-studied stick insect system, sensory inactivation of a leg disrupts proper interlimb coordination. The walking of sensory-deprived flies suggests that flies may be less dependent on sensory feedback when they walk fast because defects were more pronounced in slow flies. Stick insects walk very slowly and rarely on flat surfaces.

The writing is generally succinct and lucid, and the paper is easy to follow and interesting. The figures contain clear data but do need improvement. The work is carefully done with much detailed analysis (which provides a rich database for future workers) but there are some concerns about the analysis. FlyWalker is a nice technical innovation but some recognition of similar systems for rodents (CatWalk) should be made and an emphasis placed on the scaling to fly size. In addition to the technological innovation, the real scientific interest is the impetus to future work provided by the analysis of the sensory-deprived flies with FlyWalker. This manuscript can be accepted upon the completion of the revisions described below. The authors should give special attention to those revisions pertaining to the statistical and other analyses of the data.

Specific revisions:

1. While the writing is generally clear, there is concern that the Introduction presents a naïve view of how CPGs work and interact with descending inputs and sensory feedback. A primary interest of the manuscript is what it tells us about how sensory inputs interact with the locomotory CPG. Specifically, the opening paragraph of the manuscript should be reworked to reflect the more sophisticated view of CPGs put forth by the references cited so that this interest is properly showcased.

2. While this manuscript was under review, another manuscript concerning fly walking has been accepted for publication. Wosnitza A, Bockemühl T, Dübbert M, Scholz H, Büschges A. Inter-leg coordination in the control of walking speed in Drosophila. J Exp Biol. 2012 Oct 4. [Epub ahead of print] PubMed PMID: 23038731. This paper should be cited and the results contrasted; in particular a contrast should be made between leg amputation and sensory inactivation as a means of exploring how sensory inputs interact with the locomotory CPGs. In that manuscript all speed data are shown in body lengths so comparisons will be difficult but should nevertheless be made.

3. There were significant concerns about some of the data analysis.

a) The authors should more explicitly rationalize splitting the distribution of Figure 2A into three speed groups. (An external reviewer wrote “Although the data showing differences between fast and slow animals are fairly convincing I'd like to see a rationale for dividing the animals in 3 groups (why not 2 groups: above and below average; or 4 groups?). The way they group animals is going to affect their statistical analysis.”).

b) The distribution of Figure 2A does suggest a break at a speed of 20mm/s. Is there a statistical test that can verify this split in the distribution or can more animals at low speed be measured so that a clear break can be seen?

c) In the Materials and methods the authors state: “Because of the different behaviors of the slow, medium, and fast speed groups, some parameters were compared between individual speed groups. In these cases, the data were presented as box and whisker plots. When the data sets had a normal distribution, a Student's t-test was used. Otherwise a Mann-Whitney U-test was used.” There was general concern that these statistical methods were not adequate and that some form of ANOVA (or equivalent non-parametric test), followed by appropriate post-hoc tests, should be pursued.

d) The legend to Figure 2A states: “Speed histogram of 71 videos recorded for wild type flies, with 2 mm/s bins. Average speeds vary between 7.2 and 44.7 mm/s, with 28 mm/s the most represented speed. Although the data in this histogram fit a normal distribution, speeds of 20 mm/s are underrepresented. Additionally, both tails of the distribution curve have significant outliers (data not shown).” How were outliers determined and on what basis were they then excluded?

4. eLife allows authors to embed useful data into the text. Much of the supplemental data, including videos, can be handled in this way. The authors are encouraged to work with the editorial staff to ensure that the most fundamental supplemental data and the videos are properly integrated.
